When drafting a method/experience article, use this 7-part skeleton:

1. **Hook** — open on a real, concrete problem (the reader's or yours), not on what a tool is. The reader must see within three lines why to keep going.
2. **The problem, with numbers** — quantify the cost (time, money, effort) in specific figures, never vague ranges like "a lot of time".
3. **What you did — the method in numbered steps** — each step states what, why, and the outcome; operational enough to redo.
4. **Results, substantiated** — outcomes with numbers, and show the transparent math behind any "I saved X" claim.
5. **When NOT to use this** — mandatory. State honestly where the method fails or doesn't help. This is what makes it credible.
6. **Mistakes to avoid** — at least 3 concrete anti-patterns, each with its consequence ("if you do this, that happens").
7. **Close + optional pointer** — end without rhetoric; link any extended version soberly, as a pointer not an ad.

Title rule: no hype, no "X money in Y days". Prefer a competence claim ("what N attempts taught me about X") or a method claim ("a 4-step workflow for Y"). Add a specific one-line subtitle saying what it is and who it's for.

Pre-publish checklist: a "when NOT to use it" section exists · every number is substantiated · ≥3 documented mistakes with consequences · no guru/motivational tone or guaranteed-results promises · no private data you don't want public · the title holds up without inflating.
