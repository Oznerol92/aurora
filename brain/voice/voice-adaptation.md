When the same piece exists in two languages, treat one as the **master** and the other as an **adaptation**, never a mechanical translation.

When one language is the author's stronger one, draft in it first — brainstorm, structure, first draft flow naturally there. Then adapt into the other language with the liberty to: reformulate sentences that would sound rigid as a calque, use constructions natural to the target language when they're more direct than a literal rendering, and keep the meaning and argument while modulating the form. The adaptation can be _better_ than the master in its own language precisely because it isn't translated.

**Backport rule** (when the master is revised after the adaptation is already published, decide change-by-change):

- Fact-correction (numbers, data, references) → **always backport**.
- Conceptual clarification → backport **only if** the same passage was also confusing in the other version.
- Precise technical reformulation → backport if the precision is missing in the other version too.
- Pure stylistic preference (more formal, more articulated) → **do NOT backport** if the other version is already voice-compliant.

Operational implication: when asked to "align the other language after I revised the master," never apply changes wholesale. Diagnose the type of change, apply the rule, and state the decision transparently, case by case.
